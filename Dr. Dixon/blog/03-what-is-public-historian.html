What Is a Public Historian — and Why Does It Matter?

February 12, 2026 · 13 min read

When I introduce myself at conferences, community events, and public speaking engagements, I often say that I am a public historian. This description is usually met with polite nods—and sometimes with looks of genuine confusion. What, exactly, is a public historian? How does it differ from a "regular" historian? Why should anyone outside of academic circles care about this distinction?

These are excellent questions. And the answers illuminate something fundamental about how knowledge is created, who gets to tell stories about the past, and whose history matters enough to be remembered. The distinction between academic historians and public historians is not merely professional; it is political. It reflects different commitments about what history is for, who it serves, and how it shapes our understanding of ourselves and our world.

Understanding Public History: A Working Definition

Public history, as a discipline, is the practice of history outside of academic institutions and primarily outside of academic publishing. While academic historians are primarily trained to conduct research and write for other scholars, public historians conduct research and communicate their findings to the general public. Public historians work in museums, archives, documentary film production, community history projects, historic site interpretation, curriculum development, historical consulting, and many other venues where historical knowledge needs to be accessible to people who are not professional historians.

But this definition, while technically accurate, is incomplete. It suggests that public history is merely academic history translated into simpler language for popular consumption. In reality, public history is a distinct methodology with its own theoretical frameworks, its own ethical commitments, and its own kinds of expertise. Public historians ask different questions than academic historians. They employ different research methods. They have different relationships to their audiences and to the communities whose histories they are telling.

The National Council on Public History, the professional organization for public historians, emphasizes that public history is not secondary or derivative. It is a legitimate field of historical practice with its own rigor, its own standards, and its own contributions to historical knowledge. A museum exhibition is not a simplified version of an academic book. A documentary is not a popularization of an academic article. These are distinct ways of doing history that communicate meaning, context, and significance in ways that are appropriate to their mediums and their audiences.

Public Historians vs. Academic Historians: Understanding the Difference

The relationship between public history and academic history is complex. The two exist in a state of productive tension. Both are legitimate forms of historical practice. Both contribute to our understanding of the past. But they have different institutional homes, different funding structures, different audiences, and different professional incentives. Understanding these differences helps explain why public history is essential.

Academic historians are primarily employed by universities and research institutions. Their professional success is measured primarily through peer-reviewed publication, citation in other scholarly works, grant funding, and reputation within their disciplinary community. Academic historians tend to specialize narrowly in specific time periods or regions. They are expected to contribute to the theoretical and methodological advancement of their field. Their work is typically published in academic journals and university presses, read primarily by other scholars and advanced students.

Public historians work in a more diverse array of institutions and organizational contexts. They might be employed by museums, historical societies, state historical agencies, documentary production companies, government agencies, nonprofit organizations, or educational institutions. Their professional success is measured by different metrics: visitor engagement, community impact, educational outcomes, preservation of historical materials, and public understanding of history. Public historians are often generalists who need to understand many time periods, regions, and themes. Their work is designed to be accessible and meaningful to people without advanced training in history.

These differences are not merely stylistic. They reflect fundamentally different approaches to what history is for. Academic history, at its best, deepens our understanding of complex historical processes and tests our assumptions about how the past shapes the present. Public history, at its best, makes historical knowledge accessible to everyone, helps communities understand their own stories, and contributes to civic engagement and informed decision-making.

Public history is not secondary or derivative. It is a legitimate field of historical practice with its own rigor, its own standards, and its own contributions to historical knowledge.

The Work of Public Historians: Multiple Forms and Functions

Public history encompasses a surprising range of work. Most people are familiar with museums and historical sites. A museum curator is a public historian who researches historical topics, interprets objects and documents, designs exhibitions, and helps visitors understand significance and context. Museum work requires skills in collection management, exhibition design, education, and public communication. A curator at a history museum is doing rigorous historical research but expressing it through objects, artifacts, photographs, and interpretive text rather than through academic articles.

Archivists are public historians who organize, preserve, and make accessible historical documents and materials. Archival work is deeply technical—it requires understanding preservation science, metadata standards, and digital technology. But it is also fundamentally about making history available. An archivist makes it possible for researchers, community members, and the general public to discover and use historical sources. Without archivists, historical materials would deteriorate or remain inaccessible. Archives are the essential infrastructure that makes historical research possible.

Documentary filmmakers are public historians who use film as a medium to tell historical stories. Documentary history can communicate in ways that written history cannot—through visual imagery, sound, interview footage, and narrative cinema techniques. A well-made documentary can reach audiences who might never read a history book but who will engage deeply with historical narratives presented through film.

Public history educators develop curricula, create educational materials, and lead workshops and classes for schools and community organizations. This work is essential for helping young people understand their own histories and develop historical thinking skills. Public historians in educational contexts are not just transmitting information; they are helping people develop the intellectual tools to understand how the past shapes the present and to recognize how historical narratives have been constructed and by whom.

Community historians work with specific communities—geographic, ethnic, religious, or defined by shared experience—to research and document their own histories. This work is particularly important for marginalized communities whose histories have been systematically excluded from dominant historical narratives. A community history project might result in a published book, a digital archive, a exhibition, oral history collection, or any combination of these. The key is that it prioritizes the community's own understanding of its history, often employing community members as researchers and as authorities on their own past.

Historic site interpreters work at places of historical significance—battlefields, homes of historical figures, archaeological sites—helping visitors understand what happened there and why it matters. This work requires deep knowledge of a specific place and its history, combined with the ability to make that history come alive for diverse audiences of different ages and backgrounds.

Why Public History Matters: The Case for Historical Knowledge in Democratic Societies

Public history exists because history matters—not as an academic subject, but as something essential to how we understand ourselves, our communities, and our world. A functioning democracy requires informed citizens who can think critically about the past and the present. Public history makes that possible by making historical knowledge accessible to everyone.

Consider the role of public history in understanding current political and social issues. Understanding mass incarceration requires understanding the history of slavery, Jim Crow, and the carceral state. Understanding wealth inequality requires understanding the history of slavery, colonialism, and theft of Native American lands. Understanding immigration policy requires understanding the history of immigration restriction and xenophobia. Understanding contemporary racism requires understanding the history of structural racism. Public history helps citizens understand these connections.

Public history is also essential for community identity and solidarity. When communities understand their own histories—particularly communities that have been marginalized and whose stories have been suppressed—that historical knowledge becomes a source of pride, resilience, and collective power. A community that knows its history of resistance and survival is a community that understands its own capacity to resist and survive in the present.

This is particularly true for African American communities, Indigenous communities, immigrant communities, and other communities whose histories have been systematically excluded from mainstream historical narratives. For these communities, public history is not a luxury or an academic pursuit. It is an act of reclamation and self-determination. It is a way of asserting that our stories matter, that our ancestors' struggles and achievements are worth remembering, and that our communities deserve to see ourselves reflected in the historical record.

AHRA: Public History in Practice

My own work as founder of the Archival and Historical Research Associates (AHRA) exemplifies public history in practice. AHRA was founded on the conviction that African American history—particularly the history of African Americans in Florida, and particularly the history of the Black Seminoles—had been systematically erased from historical memory, and that this erasure was not accidental but part of a larger pattern of suppressing African American resistance and achievement.

AHRA's work combines archival research with public engagement. We research historical questions using primary sources, documentary evidence, and scholarly methods. But we do not stop at publication in academic journals. We write books intended for general audiences. We give public lectures and keynote addresses. We develop curriculum materials for schools. We work with communities to help them understand and commemorate their own histories. We create exhibits and public programs. This is public history—research conducted with rigor and intellectual honesty, but always with an eye toward making that research meaningful and accessible to the public.

AHRA embodies a particular commitment within public history: the commitment to recover and center the histories of communities whose stories have been suppressed. This work is not politically neutral. It is intentionally designed to challenge dominant historical narratives that minimize African American agency and resistance. It is designed to help African American communities understand our own histories more fully. And it is designed to help all Americans understand that the history of this nation cannot be accurately told without centering the experiences, struggles, and achievements of African Americans.

Public history is not a luxury or an academic pursuit. It is an act of reclamation and self-determination—a way of asserting that our stories matter.

Becoming a Public Historian: Paths and Possibilities

For people interested in public history as a career path, there are multiple entry points. Some public historians earn PhDs in history and deliberately choose careers outside of academia. Some earn master's degrees in public history or history specifically designed for public history careers. Some begin in related fields—library science, museum studies, archives management—and develop historical expertise as they work. Some come to public history through passionate engagement with a particular historical topic or community.

Public history positions require a different mix of skills than academic positions. While subject matter expertise is essential, equally important are skills in project management, community engagement, grant writing, digital technology, and education. A museum director needs to understand both the history they are interpreting and the practical realities of running an institution. An archivist needs to understand both historical materials and digital asset management systems. A documentary filmmaker needs to understand both history and filmmaking craft.

For people passionate about history and committed to making historical knowledge accessible, public history offers meaningful work that connects directly to communities and to civic engagement. It is work that matters. And it is work that is desperately needed in communities across the country that have been denied access to their own histories.

Challenges and the Future of Public History

Public history faces real challenges, particularly in the contemporary moment. Funding for museums, historical societies, and cultural institutions is perpetually limited. Many public history positions are part-time, poorly paid, and come without benefits or job security. The archival and preservation work that is essential to future historical research is chronically underfunded. The educational mission of public history—helping the general public understand history—is often squeezed out by the need to generate revenue and serve tourists rather than serve the broader educational mission.

There is also a broader political challenge. In recent years, there has been increased pressure on public historians to avoid telling accurate histories of racism, colonialism, and injustice. Some political actors want history to be a simple story of national progress and achievement. They resist accurate histories that center the experiences of marginalized communities. Public historians committed to truth and intellectual honesty sometimes face real pressure and censorship as they try to tell complete and accurate histories.

Despite these challenges, the work of public history is more essential than ever. In a moment of profound political and social division, when Americans increasingly lack shared understanding of our nation's history, the work of making history accessible, accurate, and meaningful becomes even more important. Public historians are essential to a functioning democracy.

Conclusion: The Power of Historical Knowledge

Public history is not a simplified version of academic history. It is a legitimate and essential form of historical practice with its own methodology, its own standards, and its own profound importance. Public historians recover suppressed histories, make historical knowledge accessible to everyone, help communities understand their own stories, and contribute to informed civic participation. This is vital work.

If you are passionate about history and about making a difference in your community, public history is a field worth considering. If you want to support the recovery and preservation of your community's history, support public historians and public institutions like museums, archives, and historical societies. And if you want to understand your own world more deeply, engage with public history. Visit museums. Read public history books. Watch documentaries. Listen to public historians speak. Discover the stories of your own community and the communities that surround you.

History is not something that happened long ago. It is the foundation of everything happening now. And public history—the work of making that history accessible, meaningful, and true—is essential work in any society committed to freedom, justice, and truth.
