A Speaker's Guide to Black History Month Programming That Lasts All Year

May 7, 2026 · 9 min read

Every February, something remarkable happens across American institutions. Corporate conference rooms fill with renewed energy. Museums mount special exhibitions. Schools schedule assembly programs. Universities host symposiums. On the surface, it looks like a national commitment to honoring Black American history. But for many organizations, this annual ritual masks a troubling reality: Black history programming becomes essentially invisible for the remaining ten months of the year.

As a public historian who has spent decades working with educational institutions and organizations nationwide, I've observed a pattern that demands examination. Too many organizations treat Black History Month as a standalone event rather than a gateway to sustained engagement with African American history. The result is programming that feels performative—what scholars call "diversity theater"—rather than transformative.

If you're an organization planning Black History Month programs, this moment offers an opportunity. You can choose a different path. You can create programming that genuinely educates, that engages your community meaningfully, and that extends far beyond February. This guide outlines how.

The Tokenism Trap: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Let me start with what doesn't work. Over the years, I've watched well-intentioned organizations fall into predictable pitfalls that actually undermine their diversity and inclusion goals.

Mistake One: The Celebrity Approach. Many organizations book famous athletes, musicians, or entertainers for Black History Month and call it educational programming. While these figures may have interesting personal stories, they're not necessarily equipped to provide historical context or scholarly insight. You end up with inspiration without education—uplifting perhaps, but not transformative. The audience leaves feeling good but not necessarily informed.

Mistake Two: Surface-Level Topics. Programming that focuses exclusively on celebrating individual achievement without examining systemic barriers, historical injustice, or structural change sends a limiting message. It says: "Here are some Black people who succeeded despite the odds," rather than "Here's how American systems were built, who benefited, and what change looks like." The first is inspirational; the second is educational.

Mistake Three: Isolation from Core Missions. I've visited organizations where Black History Month programming exists entirely separate from the institution's regular work. A museum might host a special exhibition in February while their permanent galleries remain overwhelmingly white. A corporation might host a speakers' series in Black history while their hiring practices and promotion pathways tell a different story. These disconnects are visible to your audience. They signal that Black history is supplementary, not integral.

Mistake Four: One-Off Events. Single lectures, isolated workshops, or standalone performances create a "check the box" mentality. Meaningful change in how communities understand history requires sustained engagement, repeated exposure to diverse perspectives, and time for reflection and dialogue.

Mistake Five: Ignoring Local History. Many organizations book national speakers to discuss national figures while local African American histories—the civil rights activists in your city, the historic Black neighborhoods in your region, the archival collections held in your community—go unexamined. Your audience connects most powerfully to history they can see, touch, and claim as their own.

Building Year-Round Programming: A Structural Approach

Creating programming that lasts all year requires intentional structural planning. Here's a framework that works:

Phase One: Audit and Assess. Before you plan a single program, ask hard questions. What does your organization's engagement with African American history look like currently? How diverse are your permanent collections, exhibitions, or educational materials? Who makes decisions about programming? What stories are you telling, and what stories are you missing? What does your community actually need to learn?

This audit should involve consultation with local historians, community leaders, and the Black community members you serve. Don't assume you know what's needed.

Phase Two: Develop a Year-Long Calendar. Rather than concentrating all efforts on February, distribute programming throughout the year. This might mean a lecture series running quarterly, monthly community conversations, regular exhibition rotations, or integration of African American history into your existing programming calendar. The key is consistency and visibility.

Phase Three: Diversify Formats. Not everyone learns the same way. A robust program might include: scholarly lectures for intellectually curious audiences, community conversations for dialogue and exchange, hands-on workshops for younger learners, exhibitions and visual displays, oral history projects, archival workshops, film screenings with discussion, walking tours of historic neighborhoods, and collaborative projects with community organizations.

Phase Four: Partner with Scholars, Not Just Celebrities. Invite public historians, university researchers, and specialists in specific topics to lead substantive programming. These scholars can provide context, answer complex questions, and elevate the intellectual level of your programming. They can also help your organization understand which stories matter most to tell.

Choosing Substantive Topics and Themes

What should your programming cover? This depends partly on your organization's mission, but several principles apply universally:

First, move beyond the "Greatest Hits" approach. Yes, celebrate Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman, but also explore the thousands of freedom seekers whose names we'll never know. Examine the role of white abolitionists and the contradictions within the movement. Discuss how the Underground Railroad actually functioned as a network, not a destination. Ask hard questions about complicated figures and uncomfortable truths.

Second, connect history to current events. How does understanding the history of redlining inform discussions about housing inequality today? How does studying the Great Migration illuminate current patterns of demographic change? How do historical examples of resistance and resilience speak to contemporary social movements? These connections make history relevant.

Third, include local history prominently. Every community has African American history worth telling. Research your city's Black neighborhoods, the people who built them, what happened to them, and what remains. Interview long-time residents. Work with local historians and archivists. Help your community see itself in history.

Fourth, don't shy away from difficult topics. Slavery, racism, violence, systemic injustice—these are central to American history. Audiences can handle complexity and nuance. In fact, they deserve it. Educational programming that whitewashes or sanitizes history does everyone a disservice.

The Role of Public Historians and Educational Consultants

This is where I come in—and organizations like mine exist for exactly this reason. A public historian can help you:

Develop a comprehensive programming strategy that aligns with your mission and serves your community's actual needs. Design specific programs around substantive topics. Identify and vet potential speakers and educators. Create educational materials that accompany your programs. Help integrate African American history into your permanent programming, not just special events. Consult on exhibition design and interpretation. Facilitate community conversations to ensure your programming remains responsive to community interests.

Working with an educational consultant isn't about outsourcing your commitment to diversity. It's about bringing expertise and external perspective to ensure your programming is excellent and impactful. It's an investment in doing this work well.

Creating Lasting Educational Impact

The ultimate measure of successful programming isn't attendance numbers or social media engagement. It's impact. Did your audience's understanding of history deepen? Did they make new connections between past and present? Will they continue exploring these topics after the program ends? Does your programming reach those who most need to encounter different historical narratives?

To maximize impact, consider:

Documentation and Resource Creation. Preserve your programs through recordings, transcripts, or published materials. Make them available for future learning. A lecture series becomes a permanent educational resource.

Community Partnerships. Work with schools, libraries, community centers, and churches to extend your reach beyond your immediate audience. Share resources. Co-create programming.

Evaluation and Adjustment. Gather feedback from participants. Ask what they learned, what they want to explore further, what they'd like to see more of. Use this to continuously improve your offerings.

Leadership Development. Create opportunities for community members to become educators themselves. Train community docents, facilitate discussion groups, support amateur historians in sharing their research.

Moving Forward

Black History Month can be a beginning rather than an ending point. It can be the time when your organization renews its commitment to authentic engagement with African American history—not as a special initiative, but as central to your mission and identity.

The question isn't whether you can afford to create year-round programming. It's whether you can afford not to. Communities that understand their full history—including the contributions, struggles, and wisdom of African Americans—are stronger, more connected, and more capable of addressing contemporary challenges.

If you're ready to rethink your organization's approach to Black history programming, I'd welcome the conversation. Meaningful programming requires vision, planning, and expertise. Let's build something that truly lasts.
