How Archival Research Can Save a Community's History

May 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Last year, I received a phone call that crystallized everything I believe about archival research and community history. A woman in her seventies called to say she was dying and wanted her mother's stories preserved before it was too late. Her mother had lived through Jim Crow in the rural South, had been part of migration networks, had survived by wit and resilience. But she had never written anything down. Her stories lived only in memory, and soon they would be gone.

We created an oral history project. We recorded her mother's voice. We documented her experiences, her insights, her wisdom. We created something that will endure. That something—that testimony, that archive—is preservation in its most essential form.

Archives aren't just dusty repositories in academic institutions. Archives are how communities claim and keep their own histories. Archives are how stories that powerful institutions want forgotten manage to survive. Archives are how marginalized people assert: we were here, we mattered, we deserve to be remembered. This is the true power of archival research.

What Are Archives and Why Do They Matter?

Let me start with basics. Archives are collections of documents, records, photographs, audio recordings, and materials that preserve the evidence of human experience. They're organized, cataloged, and made accessible so researchers can find and use them.

Most people associate archives with large institutions: the Library of Congress, the National Archives, university special collections. These are important, but they're not the whole story. Archives exist everywhere. They're in local historical societies, churches, community centers, libraries, and private collections. They're in people's attics—photograph albums, letters, journals, family documents.

Archives matter because they're the foundation of historical knowledge. Without primary sources—without the actual documents, records, and testimonies of the past—history becomes speculation or mythology. Archives provide evidence. They allow researchers to build arguments grounded in reality rather than assumption. They create accountability: you can verify what actually happened, rather than relying on incomplete or biased narratives.

But archives matter for another reason too, one that's essential for communities. Archives are how people claim their own history. When a community's stories are preserved in archives it controls, that community has authority over its own narrative. Archives are power.

The Problem: How Histories Get Lost and Destroyed

The history that survives in archives is, fundamentally, the history that was deemed worth keeping by people with power and resources. For centuries, the histories that got preserved were those of wealthy, prominent, white, male figures. Histories of marginalized communities—African Americans, Native Americans, immigrants, working-class people, rural communities—were systematically excluded or destroyed.

This happened in multiple ways. Sometimes it was deliberate. After slave rebellions, white authorities destroyed documents that referenced resistance. During periods of racial violence and segregation, records that might document injustice were hidden or eliminated. Political prisoners' writings disappeared. The letters of unknown people seemed worthless to those making preservation decisions.

Sometimes it was simply neglect. No institution saw value in preserving a working-class family's papers. No one thought the business records of a Black-owned enterprise in the segregated South were worth keeping. No one invested resources in collecting the oral histories of people without prominence or wealth. When people of marginal social status died, their papers were often discarded.

Fires, floods, and deterioration claimed their own share of records. Before modern preservation techniques, documents were vulnerable to the elements. Libraries and archives burned. Important records were stored in basements and destroyed by water damage. Without proper conservation, photographs faded and documents crumbled.

And sometimes, there's active erasure. Organizations that closed—churches, Black-owned businesses, civil rights groups—took their records with them when they shuttered. Records stored in private hands remained inaccessible, unavailable to researchers who might have used them to tell fuller stories.

The result is a historical record that's incomplete, biased, and shaped by who had the power to decide what mattered. Entire communities, entire dimensions of American experience, remain poorly documented.

Archives as Lifelines: Stories of Preservation

But archives also demonstrate remarkable resilience and redemptive power. Let me share a few examples from my own research:

The Narrative of Gullah Stories. For generations, the history of Gullah communities in the coastal Southeast was poorly documented in mainstream archives. But when researchers began deliberately collecting oral histories—sitting with elders, recording their voices, preserving their language and knowledge—the documentary record transformed. Archives built specifically to hold these materials mean that Gullah culture and history will be accessible to future generations.

Black Business Records. Archives like the Amistad Research Center at Tulane actively collect records from Black-owned businesses and organizations that mainstream institutions ignored for decades. These records now provide crucial evidence of Black economic agency, entrepreneurship, and institutional development during Jim Crow and beyond.

Photograph Collections. Community members across the country have donated family photographs to institutions and archives. These images—street scenes, family gatherings, church events, everyday moments—provide visual documentation of Black community life that professional photographers often ignored. They reveal what people actually looked like, how they gathered, what mattered to them.

Letters and Personal Papers. When researchers and archivists make deliberate efforts to collect and preserve personal correspondence—letters from ordinary people describing their lives, their struggles, their hopes—they create an invaluable resource. A mother's letters to a child who migrated north. A grandmother's notes about family recipes and remedies. These materials seem modest but they're irreplaceable documentation of lived experience.

Oral History Projects. Across the country, communities are recording the testimonies of elders before their knowledge disappears. These oral histories—recorded, transcribed, and archived—preserve not just information but the voices themselves. Researchers and descendants can hear how their ancestors sounded, what they emphasized, how they told their stories.

The Role of Archival Research and Consulting

This is where professional archivists and researchers come in. Building and maintaining archives requires expertise. It requires knowing how to properly preserve materials, how to organize them so they're findable, how to make them accessible while protecting privacy, how to digitize without losing essential information, how to manage institutional collections responsibly.

An archival consultant can help communities and institutions:

Assess What Needs Preserving. What materials in your community are at risk? What stories are being lost? What documents exist but are currently inaccessible? A professional assessment identifies preservation priorities.

Develop Collection Strategies. Should you focus on a specific time period, community, or topic? What types of materials are you seeking? How will you encourage people to donate? A well-developed strategy maximizes impact and ensures coherence.

Establish Proper Preservation Standards. Documents need to be stored in appropriate conditions—temperature, humidity, lighting all matter for longevity. Materials need proper housing and handling. Digitization requires specific standards to ensure quality. Professional archivists know these requirements.

Create Accessible Finding Aids. An archive is only useful if people can find what they're looking for. This requires thoughtful cataloging and description. It requires understanding researchers' needs and organizing materials accordingly.

Navigate Legal and Ethical Issues. What permissions do you need to hold materials? How do you handle sensitive information or materials that involve privacy concerns? What responsibilities do you have to donors and communities? These questions require careful thought and expertise.

Digitize and Share Responsibly. Digital access multiplies an archive's impact, but it raises new questions. What should be digitized? What restrictions are necessary? How do you ensure materials remain accessible long-term as technology changes? Professionals navigate these complexities.

How AHRA Supports Community Archives

This is work that my organization, the Archival and Historical Research Associates, is dedicated to. We work with museums, libraries, community organizations, and individual communities to build and strengthen their archival programs. We help assess collection needs, develop preservation strategies, provide training in archival standards, and consult on digitization projects.

We believe that communities deserve control over their own histories. We're committed to supporting institutions and organizations—especially those serving marginalized communities—in building archives that will preserve their stories for generations to come.

What Communities Can Do

If you're concerned about preserving your community's history, you don't need to wait for experts. You can start today:

Identify Materials at Risk. Look around your community. What papers, photographs, documents exist? Are they being cared for? Are they at risk of loss? Start a conversation about preservation.

Document Family History. Interview elders. Record their stories. Preserve family documents. These materials, shared with community archives or institutions, become part of the permanent record.

Support Local Institutions. Your library, historical society, or museum likely has archival ambitions. Support them. Donate materials. Volunteer. Help ensure these institutions have resources to do preservation work.

Partner with Professionals. If you're building an archive, work with trained archivists. The expertise is worth the investment. Professional guidance ensures your materials are preserved responsibly and made accessible for maximum impact.

Make Archives Accessible. Preserve materials not just for scholars but for community members. Create programming around your collections. Make research easy. Ensure that the history you preserve actually reaches the people who most need to know it.

The Legacy of Preserved History

When we preserve a community's history through archival research, we're not just creating a resource for scholars. We're affirming that the lives of ordinary people matter. We're creating evidence that can contradict dominant narratives. We're providing resources that communities can use to understand themselves, to claim their agency, to plan their futures informed by their pasts.

A young person who discovers archival evidence that their grandmother was an activist, their community was thriving before disinvestment, their ancestors possessed knowledge and resilience—that discovery changes something. It shifts perspective. It creates pride and agency.

Archives are resistance. Archives are memory. Archives are how communities ensure they will not be forgotten. If you care about preserving the histories that institutions overlook, archival research is where you start.
