A Brief History of African Americans in Florida

February 26, 2026 · 15 min read

Florida's history is African American history. From the earliest days of European colonization through the contemporary moment, African Americans have shaped Florida's political, economic, cultural, and social landscape. Yet this history has been systematically suppressed, erased, and rewritten to minimize African American agency and maximize the achievements of white Floridians. To understand Florida—its past, its present, and its possibilities—we must center the stories, struggles, and triumphs of African Americans who have lived, worked, resisted, and thrived in this land.

This essay offers a brief overview of African American history in Florida from the Spanish colonial era to the present. It cannot possibly cover all the complexity and richness of this history. Rather, it aims to provide a framework for understanding the continuities and transformations that have characterized the African American experience in Florida across nearly five centuries.

The Spanish Colonial Period: Freedom and Bondage in Early Florida

African Americans have lived in Florida since the earliest days of European colonization. The first Europeans to arrive in Florida brought African slaves with them. But Florida's Spanish colonial regime operated under different assumptions about slavery and race than the later Anglo-American plantation system. Spanish law recognized slaves' right to purchase freedom, to maintain family relationships, and to protect their children from inherited slavery. These legal protections, combined with the economic realities of colonial Florida, created a situation where free African Americans and people of color formed a significant portion of Florida's population.

The most famous example of Spanish colonial Florida's treatment of African Americans is the founding of Fort Mose, located near St. Augustine, in 1738. Fort Mose was established as a settlement for free and formerly enslaved African Americans who had fled slavery in the English colonies. The Spanish welcomed these refugees, recognizing both the humanitarian value of offering asylum and the strategic advantage of accepting people fleeing English territory. At Fort Mose, African Americans built a community, served in the Spanish military, and lived in a condition that, while not perfect equality, represented a remarkable departure from the slavery and racial oppression they had escaped.

Fort Mose's existence is extraordinarily significant. It represents the first free African American settlement in North America, established more than a century before Haitian independence and more than a century and a half before African American freedom became the law of the United States. That Fort Mose is rarely mentioned in American history textbooks, despite its historical importance, illustrates how systematically African American achievement in colonial Florida has been erased from historical memory.

The Spanish colonial period also saw the development of substantial communities of free people of color—gente de color libres—throughout Florida. These included people of African descent, people of Native American and African descent, and people of mixed European and African descent. These communities maintained their own social structures, conducted their own commerce, and participated in colonial society in ways that would be impossible after the British conquest and particularly after American independence and the rise of the plantation system.

The Black Seminoles: A Nation Resisting Enslavement

One of the most important chapters in Florida African American history is the history of the Black Seminoles—a distinct nation of free and enslaved African Americans who lived among the Seminole Nation in central and southern Florida from the eighteenth century through the early nineteenth century. The Black Seminoles represent a remarkable example of African American resistance, autonomy, and community formation in the face of chattel slavery.

As discussed more extensively in my earlier essay on this topic, the Black Seminoles had established prosperous communities throughout Florida by the early nineteenth century. They maintained their own leadership structures, their own economic systems, and a degree of autonomy remarkable in the context of American slavery. The United States government, recognizing the threat that free and semi-free African Americans posed to the slavery system, launched a series of military campaigns—the Seminole Wars—whose primary purpose was to recapture and re-enslave the Black Seminole population.

The Second Seminole War (1835–1842) was the longest and costliest Indian War in American history, fought primarily to recapture the Black Seminoles. Despite devastating military pressure, Black Seminole warriors and their Seminole allies resisted for seven years. Eventually, most were forced to exile in Indian Territory. But their resistance left a permanent mark on Florida history—evidence that African Americans in Florida had actively resisted enslavement, had achieved military victories against the United States military, and had maintained their dignity and freedom for as long as possible.

Slavery and the Antebellum Period: The Expansion of Plantation Slavery

Following the American acquisition of Florida in 1821 and particularly after Indian removal, Florida underwent a dramatic transformation. Spanish law and Spanish concepts of race relations were replaced with Anglo-American slavery—one of the harshest and most dehumanizing systems ever devised. The plantation system expanded, slavery became the dominant labor system, and Florida's Black population grew dramatically as enslaved people were forcibly brought from the Carolinas and Georgia to work in Florida's developing plantations.

The antebellum period was catastrophic for African Americans in Florida. Enslavement expanded. Free African American communities that had existed under Spanish rule were restricted and marginalized. The legal status of African Americans was reduced to a state of permanent subordination. Enslaved people in Florida worked on cotton plantations, sugar plantations, and in naval stores production. They endured the full brutality of American chattel slavery—family separation, torture, sexual violence, and the constant threat of death.

Yet even during this darkest period, African Americans resisted. They escaped slavery, seeking refuge with remaining Seminole communities or in other states. They participated in the Civil War when the opportunity came. They maintained their families and their communities under extraordinary duress. They preserved their humanity and dignity in the face of systems designed to strip both away.

Reconstruction and Redemption: Brief Freedom, Then Terrorism

The Civil War and Reconstruction period offered a brief moment when African Americans in Florida could envision real freedom. With the end of slavery came the possibility of political participation. The Reconstruction period saw African American men voting, holding political office, and exercising unprecedented political power. Florida's first Reconstruction governors included supporters of equal rights. Schools were established for African American children. Land redistribution programs, though limited, provided some formerly enslaved people with the possibility of owning property.

But this period of promise was short-lived. As Reconstruction ended and white Southern Democrats regained political control, they unleashed a campaign of terror designed to strip African Americans of their political rights and establish white supremacy. This period—often called "Redemption" in historical literature that supported white Southern politics, but more accurately understood as a campaign of white terrorism—was marked by violence, intimidation, and the systematic elimination of African American political participation.

Two specific incidents from this period deserve emphasis: the Ocoee massacre of 1920 and the Rosewood massacre of 1923. Both were pogroms in which white mobs attacked African American communities, burned homes and businesses, murdered African American residents, and drove survivors into exile. These were not spontaneous riots. They were organized campaigns of racial terrorism designed to maintain white supremacy and punish African Americans for attempts at economic self-determination and political participation.

The Ocoee massacre occurred in Orange County on Election Day 1920. White mobs attacked the African American community in response to African American voters attempting to vote in the presidential election. They burned homes, murdered residents, and effectively destroyed one of the most prosperous African American communities in Florida. The exact death toll is unknown but is certainly more than the official count of around two people—likely hundreds.

The Rosewood massacre occurred in Levy County in January 1923. White mobs from surrounding communities attacked the town of Rosewood in response to an accusation of sexual assault involving an African American man and a white woman (an accusation that was almost certainly false). The mobs burned Rosewood to the ground, killed at least eight African American residents (likely many more), and drove the surviving African American residents into permanent exile. Rosewood, a thriving African American community, was completely destroyed.

The Ocoee and Rosewood massacres were not spontaneous riots. They were organized campaigns of racial terrorism designed to maintain white supremacy and punish African Americans for economic self-determination.

Jim Crow Segregation: The Long Night of Apartheid

The period from the 1920s through the 1960s was characterized by comprehensive Jim Crow segregation in Florida. While Florida did not have slavery, it had something comparably oppressive: a complete system of racial apartheid enforced through law, violence, and social custom. African Americans and whites were systematically separated in housing, education, employment, public accommodations, transportation, and virtually every other aspect of life. African American schools were dramatically underfunded compared to white schools. African American neighborhoods were concentrated in areas excluded from property ownership opportunities, economic development, and public investment. African American workers were confined to the lowest-paid, most dangerous, and most degrading occupations. African American political participation was suppressed through intimidation and legal manipulation.

During this period, Florida's economy was developing. Tourism was growing. Miami was transforming from a small settlement into a major metropolitan area. But African Americans were largely excluded from the benefits of economic growth. They provided essential labor as service workers, agricultural laborers, and construction workers, but they were not allowed to share in the prosperity their labor created. Segregation was not just a matter of social prejudice; it was a system of economic exploitation designed to maintain white wealth accumulation and African American poverty.

Yet during this period, African American communities in Florida developed their own institutions, built their own businesses, created their own cultural and religious spaces. African American churches became centers of community life and resistance. African American entrepreneurs established businesses in segregated communities. African American musicians, artists, and intellectuals contributed to Florida's cultural life despite segregation's constraints. Miami's Overtown neighborhood became a center of African American culture and commerce. Tampa's Ybor City had a significant African American population. Jacksonville had substantial African American communities. These neighborhoods represented African American self-determination and community building under oppressive circumstances.

The Civil Rights Movement: Struggle for Equality in Florida

Florida was a significant center of civil rights activism in the 1950s and 1960s. The sit-in movement, which began in Greensboro, North Carolina, quickly spread to Florida. Young African Americans in cities across Florida—Miami, Jacksonville, Tampa, Tallahassee, and other cities—organized sit-ins at segregated lunch counters, demanding the right to eat at public accommodations. These young activists faced police brutality, arrest, and imprisonment. But their protests contributed to the national movement that eventually led to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Florida also produced important civil rights leaders. Harry T. Moore, an NAACP organizer in Brevard County, was a courageous civil rights activist who worked tirelessly for voting rights and racial equality. In 1951, Moore and his wife were killed by a bomb planted by white supremacists—one of the first murders of a civil rights activist by a white supremacist organization during the modern civil rights era. Moore's martyrdom became a symbol of the movement's determination and sacrifice.

The movement for school desegregation was also significant in Florida. Despite the Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education declaring segregation in schools unconstitutional, Florida and other Southern states resisted integration. Segregation academies—private schools established specifically to avoid integration—opened throughout Florida. But African American parents and civil rights activists continued to fight for desegregation, knowing that segregated education meant inferior education for African American children.

By the late 1960s, segregation was legally dismantled, though residential and economic segregation persisted and continues to persist. African Americans achieved the right to vote (though voting rights continue to be contested). Discrimination in public accommodations was declared illegal. Educational integration, though partial and inadequate, began. But these legal victories, while significant, did not eliminate the fundamental inequalities that segregation had created and that had been building since the colonial period.

The Modern Era: Inequality, Resistance, and Community

In the contemporary period, African Americans in Florida continue to struggle against racism and inequality while building powerful communities, institutions, and movements for justice. Contemporary Florida reflects both the progress of the civil rights era and the persistent legacies of slavery, segregation, and discrimination.

Florida continues to have significant racial inequality in income, wealth, education, health, and criminal justice. African Americans in Florida are disproportionately incarcerated, disproportionately poor, disproportionately subject to police violence, and disproportionately excluded from positions of economic and political power. Residential segregation, while no longer legally mandated, persists due to the cumulative effects of past discrimination combined with ongoing systemic inequality. Educational inequality persists despite integration.

Yet African American Floridians continue to build. Miami has a substantial Haitian American community that has transformed the city while also facing discrimination and police violence. Tampa, Jacksonville, and other Florida cities have vibrant African American communities with strong cultural institutions. African American voters have become increasingly politically powerful in Florida, with African American voter turnout and engagement crucial to state electoral outcomes. African American entrepreneurs continue to establish businesses and create wealth despite systemic obstacles. African American activists continue to struggle for voting rights, economic justice, police accountability, and environmental justice.

The 2020 Movement for Black Lives has resonated powerfully in Florida, with protests against police violence occurring in cities throughout the state. These protests are part of a long African American tradition of resistance to state violence and racial injustice—a tradition that includes the Black Seminole Wars, the civil rights sit-ins, and countless acts of everyday resistance and community building that characterize the African American experience in Florida.

Understanding Florida Through African American History

To truly understand Florida, one must center African American history. You cannot understand Florida's colonial past without understanding Fort Mose and the free African American communities of Spanish Florida. You cannot understand Florida's development without understanding the Black Seminole Wars. You cannot understand Florida's economy without understanding slavery and its successors. You cannot understand Florida's contemporary politics without understanding the legacy of racial terrorism, segregation, and disenfranchisement, and without recognizing the power of African American voters and activists.

Most importantly, understanding Florida through an African American lens reveals the continuous story of African American agency, resistance, and community building. This is not a story of passive victimization, though victimization is certainly part of it. It is a story of people who resisted slavery, who established free communities, who survived the worst that American racism could inflict, who fought for their rights and their dignity, and who continue to struggle and build in the contemporary moment.

The history of African Americans in Florida is the history of Florida. When we tell Florida's story accurately, we must center this history. When we teach about Florida, we must make sure that students learn about Fort Mose, about the Black Seminoles, about the Ocoee and Rosewood massacres, about the civil rights movement, and about contemporary African American communities. And when we think about Florida's future, we must recognize that it will be shaped, as it has always been shaped, by the contributions, struggles, and visions of African Americans who call Florida home.
