Preserving Black Studies in the Age of AI

April 23, 2026 · 11 min read

Artificial intelligence and advanced technology are transforming how we access, search, and understand information. For fields like Black studies and African American history, these technological changes present both extraordinary opportunities and genuine risks. On one hand, AI-powered tools can make historical archives more accessible, searchable, and discoverable than ever before. On the other hand, if not carefully managed, AI could flatten, distort, or even erase the nuance and complexity of Black scholarship and history. This moment requires urgent attention from scholars, archivists, institutions, and the broader public to ensure that technology serves—rather than subverts—the preservation and advancement of Black studies.

The Promise: AI and Digital Archives

Let's start with the genuine promise that AI and digital technology offer to Black studies and archival work. Digital archives represent a revolution in how historical materials can be preserved, organized, and accessed.

Traditionally, archival research required traveling to physical locations, accessing materials only during business hours, and working under various restrictions on how materials could be copied or used. This created barriers, particularly for people without institutional affiliations or adequate resources to travel. Digital archives break down these barriers. Historical documents, photographs, audio recordings, and other materials can be digitized and made accessible online, available 24/7 to anyone with internet access.

AI tools enhance this accessibility further. Optical character recognition (OCR) technology can convert images of handwritten or printed documents into searchable text. Machine learning can help organize and categorize vast collections of materials. Natural language processing can help researchers identify relevant documents across large databases. These technologies have the potential to make historical research faster, more efficient, and more accessible to people without formal training in archival research methods.

For Black studies specifically, digital archives are invaluable. Much of the documentary record of Black history exists in scattered locations—individual repositories, family collections, community institutions, and international archives. Digitizing and organizing these materials creates the possibility of a more complete historical record. A researcher in a rural area can now access materials from the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture. A high school student can explore archives about Black Seminole history without traveling to multiple countries. This democratization of access is genuinely transformative.

The Risks: AI, Distortion, and Erasure

But we must also be clear-eyed about the risks that AI poses to Black scholarship and accurate understanding of Black history. These risks are real and worth taking seriously.

Distortion Through Algorithmic Bias: AI systems are trained on data. If that data reflects historical biases, the AI system will reproduce and amplify those biases. Consider a machine learning system trained on historical newspaper databases to predict which topics were considered newsworthy. Those newspapers were often explicitly racist, and they covered Black people and Black history through a distorted lens. An AI system trained on that data might learn to systematically underrepresent Black perspectives and stories, or to associate Black people with crime and pathology rather than achievement and culture. Without careful oversight, such biases become embedded in the systems that now mediate our access to information.

Flattening of Nuance and Complexity: Good history requires understanding nuance. It requires understanding that historical figures and communities are complex, that narratives have multiple perspectives, that outcomes depend on specific historical contexts. AI systems, particularly large language models, tend to produce averages—they smooth out nuance in favor of prevalent patterns. For Black history, this is dangerous. The complexity of Black resistance, the diversity of Black thought, the specificity of particular communities and their histories—these are at risk of being flattened into simplified narratives by AI systems designed for average prediction rather than historical truth.

Erasure of Marginalized Perspectives: Much of Black history exists in sources that are not well-represented in digitized databases. Oral histories, private family papers, community records, and materials in non-English languages are less likely to be digitized and searchable. If AI systems are trained primarily on the materials that have been digitized and are easily searchable, they will reproduce the biases of what has been digitally preserved. Entire categories of knowledge and experience could be systematically erased.

Loss of Human Interpretation: Archival work at its best is not just about making documents accessible—it's about interpretation, contextualization, and expert curation. An archivist who specializes in Black history understands the significance of documents that a general user might overlook. They understand the networks connecting documents. They can provide context that helps researchers understand what they're looking at. When we replace human curation with algorithmic search, we risk losing this expertise and interpretation.

Intellectual Property and Academic Labor: There's another risk that particularly affects working scholars. Large language models and AI systems are trained on published works, including academic scholarship. Black scholars' work is being used to train systems that compete with those scholars for attention, funding, and credibility. There are questions about compensation, attribution, and whether scholars should have control over how their work is used to train AI systems. For a field like Black studies, where scholars often labor for causes larger than individual advancement, questions about intellectual property become questions about power and voice.

The Importance of Human Curation in Black Studies

This is where human curation and expertise become crucial. The most valuable digital archives are not just searchable databases—they are thoughtfully organized, contextualized collections created by people who understand the significance of the materials and the communities they document.

Expert archivists, librarians, and scholars bring irreplaceable knowledge. They understand which documents matter, how they relate to each other, what context they need. They can make decisions about how materials are described, organized, and presented that respect the dignity and humanity of the people documented in those materials. They can ensure that materials are presented with appropriate historical context and analysis.

This is particularly important for materials related to African American history and Black communities. The history of archives shows that Black people's history has often been documented from the perspective of those in power—written in records of enslavement, arrest, surveillance, and control rather than in records of Black people's own words and actions. An important part of contemporary archival work is finding and centering sources created by and about Black people on their own terms, and providing context that challenges dominant narratives.

AI systems cannot do this work of interpretation and contextualization. They can assist—by making materials searchable, by identifying patterns across collections—but the human expertise remains irreplaceable.

The Role of Public Historians in the AI Age

Public historians—scholars who make history accessible to broad audiences and who work outside traditional academic settings—have important roles to play in this moment. Public historians can:

Advocate for Inclusive Digitization: Push for efforts to digitize materials that have been marginalized or overlooked. Make the case that understanding Black history requires access to diverse sources, including materials in African American communities, community organizations, and non-English sources.

Provide Expert Context: Create explanatory materials that help the public understand digitized historical materials. This contextualization helps people interpret what they're reading and prevents misunderstanding.

Challenge Algorithmic Bias: Document instances where AI systems distort or misrepresent Black history. Help the public understand how algorithmic bias works and why it matters.

Ensure Community Control: Work with communities to ensure that their histories are documented and presented in ways that respect their dignity and reflect their perspectives. Black communities should have control over narratives about their own histories.

Advocate for Labor and Resources: Make the case for funding human archival work, archival training, and academic positions in Black studies. These resources are necessary to ensure that the human expertise required to do this work well is valued and supported.

AHRA and Archival Technology

Organizations like the Archival and Historical Research Associates (AHRA) represent a model for how technology can serve Black studies and archival work while maintaining human expertise and community accountability. AHRA uses technology as a tool—to organize materials, to make them more accessible, to preserve them for future generations—while prioritizing the expertise of historians and the voices of communities whose histories are documented.

In using technology, AHRA asks: Who benefits from this technological approach? Whose histories are made more visible? Whose are marginalized? How can technology serve communities rather than serving the interests of corporations or governments? How can we ensure that the technology enhances rather than replaces human expertise and community knowledge?

These are the right questions to be asking across archival and historical work in the age of AI.

What Real Integration of AI in Black Studies Looks Like

The goal should not be to avoid technology or AI. Rather, the goal is to integrate technology in ways that truly serve Black scholarship and the communities these fields represent.

AI as Assistant, Not Replacement: Use AI to assist archivists and historians in their work—making materials more searchable, helping identify patterns, assisting with organization—while keeping human expertise and judgment central.

Diverse Representation in Training Data: Ensure that if AI systems are used, they're trained on diverse datasets that include Black perspectives and avoid over-representing biased sources. This requires intentional work to identify and include materials that might not be as widely available.

Transparent Methodology: When AI is used in archival or historical work, be transparent about how it's being used, what biases it might introduce, and what limitations it has. Help users understand what they're getting from algorithmic search and what they might be missing.

Community Control and Consent: Ensure that communities whose histories are being digitized and made AI-searchable have control over how their materials are used and understood. Get informed consent, share benefits, respect community protocols around sensitive materials.

Investment in Human Expertise: Recognize that the most important resource for Black studies is not AI—it's expert historians, archivists, and scholars. Invest in training, in positions, in support for people doing this work.

A Future Worth Fighting For

We have the opportunity to use technology to preserve and advance Black studies in unprecedented ways. We can make Black history more accessible. We can preserve materials that might otherwise be lost. We can help new generations of researchers discover and engage with African American history and culture.

But this will only happen if we're intentional about it. If we allow technology to develop without oversight and accountability, we risk having it distort or erase Black scholarship. If we replace human expertise with algorithms, we lose the interpretation and context that makes history meaningful. If we allow technology to serve corporate or governmental interests rather than communities, we undermine the liberation work that Black studies is fundamentally about.

The work of preserving Black studies in the age of AI is work that requires historians, archivists, technology experts, and communities working together. It requires resistance to the idea that technology should simply be adopted uncritically. It requires advocacy for the resources and expertise needed to do this work well. It requires holding technology accountable to serving Black scholarship and Black communities, not the other way around.

This is the moment to make those commitments and fight for that vision.

"Technology is a tool. The question is not whether to use tools, but who controls them and what purposes they serve. In Black studies, our tools must serve truth, community, and liberation."
