The Documentary

A War They Called Indian Removal. It Was About Slavery.

From 1817 to 1858, the United States government engaged in a bitter conflict with the Seminole Nation in three distinct wars. The Second Seminole War — fought from 1835 to 1842 — was the costliest and most prolonged. Official history framed it as a conflict over land and removal policy. The truth runs deeper.

The U.S. military's primary objective in the Second Seminole War was not the relocation of the Seminole Nation. It was the capture and re-enslavement of the Black Seminoles — free Black men and women who had built sovereign communities alongside the Seminole people for generations. They had fought for their freedom before. They would fight again.

Dr. Anthony E. Dixon, whose doctoral dissertation and 2014 book Florida's Negro War is the definitive scholarly work on the subject, has spent 20 years building the research base for this story. The Black Seminole Project — a documentary and feature film — will bring it to the screen for the first time with the depth and accuracy it deserves.

The documentary traces the full arc of the Black Seminole experience: from the earliest Spanish Florida, through the wars, through removal to Oklahoma, to the communities that survive today in Bracketville, Texas, the Bahamas, and Mexico. The feature film adapts the most dramatic period — the war itself.

Why This Story

It Happened Here

In Florida. Descendants of the Black Seminoles live today in Bracketville, Texas, the Bahamas, and Mexico. This is not ancient history.

It's Being Erased

Debates over Black history in schools, sanitized textbooks, and institutional reluctance have obscured the Black Seminole story from public memory.

No One Has Told It

No major documentary or feature film has adequately captured the depth, complexity, and heroism of the Black Seminoles on screen.

The Foundation of the Project

Florida's Negro War

Dr. Dixon's 2014 book is the definitive scholarly work on the role of Black Seminoles in the Second Seminole War. Synthesizing archival research, primary sources, and oral history, Florida's Negro War establishes that the conflict was fundamentally about slavery, freedom, and resistance — not just Indian removal.

This exhaustively researched foundation becomes the basis for both the documentary and feature film, ensuring historical accuracy and scholarly rigor while making the story accessible to broad audiences.
